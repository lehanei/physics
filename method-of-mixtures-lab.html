Method of Mixtures Lab

Method of Mixtures Lab

Bodies at different temperatures share a sealed, insulated container. Energy flows from hotter to cooler until they all reach the same temperature.

Student guide

Open a section to read it. Each one ends with something to try in the lab.

1. Heat flows until the temperatures are equal

When bodies at different temperatures touch, energy flows from the hotter body to the cooler one. This energy in transit is heat. The flow stops when every body is at the same temperature. That shared temperature is the final temperature, θf.

The container is insulated, so no energy escapes. Energy is conserved, which gives the key idea of every method-of-mixtures problem:

energy lost by the hotter bodies = energy gained by the cooler bodies

TryPress Mix and watch the glowing dots. They always cross from the hotter body to the cooler one, and they stop when the temperatures match.

2. Q = mcΔθ: what the temperature change depends on

The energy needed to change the temperature of a body is

Q = mcΔθ

Rearranged for the temperature change: Δθ = Q / (mc).

  • As Q gets bigger, Δθ gets bigger (Q is above the line). Δθ ∝ Q.
  • As m gets bigger, Δθ gets smaller (m is under the line). Δθ ∝ 1/m.
  • As c gets bigger, Δθ gets smaller (c is under the line). Δθ ∝ 1/c.

Definition from the equation: c = Q / (mΔθ). Specific heat capacity is the energy needed per kilogram per kelvin of temperature change.

Units from the equation: J ÷ (kg × K) = J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

TryMix 0.5 kg of aluminium at 90 °C with 0.4 kg of water at 20 °C. The same energy leaves the aluminium as enters the water, yet the aluminium's temperature falls much further. Look at the mc values in the worked solution to see why.

3. Heat capacity, mc, decides who wins

The product mc is the heat capacity of the whole body. From mc = Q / Δθ, it is the energy needed per kelvin of temperature change. Units: J ÷ K = J K⁻¹.

Every body exchanges the same amount of energy with the others, so the body with the bigger mc changes temperature by less. The final temperature ends up closer to the starting temperature of the body with the bigger mc.

For any number of bodies, setting the total energy gained to zero gives

θf = (m₁c₁θ₁ + m₂c₂θ₂ + …) / (m₁c₁ + m₂c₂ + …)

This is an average of the starting temperatures, weighted by each body's mc.

TryDouble the mass of the water and mix again. θf moves closer to the water's starting temperature.

4. Changes of state: Q = ml

Melting ice or condensing steam involves energy but no change in temperature. The energy changes the arrangement of the particles, not how fast they move.

Q = ml
  • As m gets bigger, Q gets bigger. Q ∝ m.
  • Definition from the equation: l = Q / m. Specific latent heat is the energy needed per kilogram to change state, with no change in temperature.
  • Units from the equation: J ÷ kg = J kg⁻¹.

Ice at 0 °C must first melt (Q = mlf) and then warm as water (Q = mcwΔθ). Steam at 100 °C first condenses (Q = mlv) and then cools as water.

TryIn Find final temperature, set Body 2 (water) to −10 °C. It becomes ice. Mix it with the hot aluminium and watch the temperature of the ice stay at 0 °C while it melts.

5. The copper calorimeter

In the real experiment the water sits in a copper calorimeter. The calorimeter starts at the water's temperature and finishes at θf, so it gains (or loses) energy too:

Qcal = mcalcCuΔθ

Its mc is added to the water's mc. If you leave it out, energy is missing from your balance, so the c or l you calculate comes out too small.

Good practice in the real experiment: lag (insulate) the calorimeter and use a lid, stir the mixture, transfer the hot body quickly, and dry ice or steam before adding it.

TryIn Find c, note the answer, then press + Add copper calorimeter. The answer increases.

6. Reading the temperature–time graph

Each line shows one body. The lines move towards each other and meet at θf, shown as the dashed line.

  • The line that moves less belongs to the body with the bigger mc.
  • The lines are steepest at the start, when the temperature difference is biggest, because heat flows fastest then. They level off as the difference shrinks.
  • A body that is changing state stays flat at 0 °C or 100 °C while the change happens.

  1. Find final temperature · Body 1: aluminium, 0.5 kg, 90 °C · Body 2: water, 0.4 kg, 20 °C

    Before you press Mix, calculate the final temperature. Then press Mix to check.

    Show answer

    Energy lost by the aluminium = energy gained by the water:

    (0.5)(900)(90 − θ) = (0.4)(4180)(θ − 20)

    40 500 − 450θ = 1672θ − 33 440, so 2122θ = 73 940 and θ = 34.8 °C.

  2. Same mixture as question 1

    How much energy does the water gain? Compare it with the energy the aluminium loses, shown under the worked solution.

    kJ
    Show answer

    Q = mcΔθ = (0.4)(4180)(34.8 − 20) = 24.8 kJ.

    The aluminium loses (0.5)(900)(90 − 34.8) = 24.8 kJ. They are equal because the container is insulated and energy is conserved.

  3. Same mixture as question 1

    The aluminium's temperature changes by more than the water's. How many times bigger is the aluminium's temperature change? Explain using Δθ = Q / (mc).

    times
    Show answer

    Aluminium: Δθ = 90 − 34.8 = 55.2 K. Water: Δθ = 34.8 − 20 = 14.8 K. Ratio = 55.2 ÷ 14.8 = 3.7.

    Both exchange the same Q. In Δθ = Q / (mc), mc is under the line, so the body with the bigger mc changes temperature by less. The water's mc is 1672 J K⁻¹ and the aluminium's is 450 J K⁻¹. 1672 ÷ 450 = 3.7, the same ratio.

  4. Find c · default values: metal 0.2 kg at 100 °C, water 0.1 kg at 15 °C, θf = 28.4 °C

    (a) With no calorimeter, calculate c for the metal.

    J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹

    (b) Press + Add copper calorimeter (0.05 kg). Calculate c again, using the same θf.

    J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹

    (c) Explain why ignoring the calorimeter gives a value of c that is too small.

    Show answer

    (a) (0.2)c(100 − 28.4) = (0.1)(4180)(28.4 − 15), so c = 5601.2 ÷ 14.32 = 391 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

    (b) (0.2)c(71.6) = [(0.1)(4180) + (0.05)(390)](13.4) = 5862.5, so c = 409 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

    (c) The calorimeter also gains energy from the metal. Leaving it out undercounts the energy the metal lost. Since c = Q / (mΔθ) and Q is above the line, a smaller Q gives a smaller c.

  5. Find l → Fusion (ice) · default values: water 0.2 kg at 30 °C, ice 0.03 kg at 0 °C, θf = 15.7 °C

    Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion of ice. Press Mix and compare with the worked solution.

    J kg⁻¹
    Show answer

    Energy lost by the warm water = energy to melt the ice + energy to warm the melted ice from 0 °C to θf:

    (0.2)(4180)(30 − 15.7) = (0.03)l + (0.03)(4180)(15.7)

    11 954.8 = 0.03l + 1968.8, so l = 3.33 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹.

    Students often forget the second term. The melted ice is water at 0 °C, and it still has to warm up to θf.

  6. Find final temperature · Body 1: water, 0.2 kg, 30 °C · Body 2: water, 0.05 kg, −10 °C (ice)

    (a) Will all the ice melt? Compare the energy the ice needs to reach 0 °C and melt with the energy the warm water can give out in cooling to 0 °C. Use cice = 2100 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

    (b) Calculate the final temperature, then press Mix to check.

    °C
    Show answer

    (a) Ice needs (0.05)(2100)(10) + (0.05)(3.34 × 10⁵) = 1050 + 16 700 = 17 750 J. The water can give (0.2)(4180)(30) = 25 080 J in cooling to 0 °C. 25 080 J is more than 17 750 J, so all the ice melts.

    (b) Energy lost by the warm water = energy gained by the ice:

    (0.2)(4180)(30 − θ) = 17 750 + (0.05)(4180)(θ − 0)

    25 080 − 836θ = 17 750 + 209θ, so 1045θ = 7330 and θ = 7.0 °C.

  7. Find l → Vaporisation (steam) · default values

    Press Mix. Only 0.01 kg of steam warms 0.3 kg of water by about 20 °C. Calculate the energy given out by 0.01 kg of steam at 100 °C as it condenses and cools to body temperature, 37 °C. Use lv = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹. Then explain why a scald from steam is worse than one from boiling water.

    kJ
    Show answer

    Condensing: (0.01)(2.26 × 10⁶) = 22 600 J. Cooling: (0.01)(4180)(100 − 37) = 2633 J. Total = 25.2 kJ.

    The same mass of boiling water gives out only the 2633 J from cooling. Steam gives out about ten times as much energy onto the skin, because it releases its latent heat as it condenses.
