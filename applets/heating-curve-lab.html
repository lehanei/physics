Heating Curve Lab

Supply energy to a substance and watch what its particles do. Read the energy supplied and the temperature, record readings, and see the heating curve build up.

Student guide

Open a section to read it. Each one ends with something to try in the lab.

1. What the particles are doing
  • Solid: particles are held in fixed positions by strong bonds. They can only vibrate about those positions.
  • Liquid: particles are still close together, but they can slide past each other.
  • Gas: particles are far apart and move freely in all directions.

Temperature is a measure of how fast the particles are moving on average, that is, their average kinetic energy. Hotter particles vibrate or move faster.

TryChoose water and switch the heater on. Watch the ice particles vibrate harder as the temperature rises towards 0 °C.

2. Sloped sections: Q = mcΔθ

While the substance stays in one state, the energy supplied raises its temperature:

Q = mcΔθ

Rearranged: Δθ = Q / (mc).

  • As Q gets bigger, Δθ gets bigger (Q is above the line). Δθ ∝ Q.
  • As m or c gets bigger, Δθ gets smaller (they are under the line). Δθ ∝ 1/m and Δθ ∝ 1/c.

Definition from the equation: c = Q / (mΔθ). Specific heat capacity is the energy needed per kilogram per kelvin of temperature change.

Units from the equation: J ÷ (kg × K) = J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Because Δθ ∝ Q, each sloped section of the graph of θ against Q is a straight line. Its gradient is Δθ / Q = 1 / (mc). A bigger mc gives a shallower line.

TryCompare the slopes for ice, water and steam. Water has the biggest c (4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹), so its line is the shallowest.

3. Flat sections: Q = ml

During melting and boiling the heater is still on, but the temperature does not change. The energy breaks the bonds holding the particles together, increasing their potential energy rather than their speed.

Q = ml
  • As m gets bigger, Q gets bigger. Q ∝ m.
  • Definition from the equation: l = Q / m. Specific latent heat is the energy needed per kilogram to change state, with no change in temperature.
  • Units from the equation: J ÷ kg = J kg⁻¹.

Boiling needs far more energy than melting. Melting only loosens the bonds, but boiling separates the particles completely. So lv is much bigger than lf, and the boiling section is much longer.

TryHeat water all the way to steam. Compare the length of the flat section at 100 °C with the one at 0 °C.

4. The heater: Q = Pt

The heater supplies energy at a steady rate:

Q = Pt
  • Definition from the equation: P = Q / t. Power is the energy supplied per unit time.
  • Units from the equation: J ÷ s = J s⁻¹, which is the watt (W).
  • For a fixed P, Q ∝ t. A graph of θ against time therefore has the same shape as the graph of θ against Q.
  • As P gets bigger, the time to supply a given Q gets smaller (t = Q / P, P is under the line).

TryDouble the heater power and watch how much faster the energy meter climbs.

5. Reading the heating curve

The heating curve has five sections:

  • Rising: solid warms (Q = mcsΔθ)
  • Flat: melting at the melting point (Q = mlf)
  • Rising: liquid warms (Q = mclΔθ)
  • Flat: boiling at the boiling point (Q = mlv)
  • Rising: gas warms (Q = mcgΔθ)

To measure c, record two readings on a sloped section: c = ΔQ / (mΔθ). To measure l, record a reading at the start and end of a flat section: l = ΔQ / m.

Use the lab to answer each question. Type numbers like 3.34 × 10⁵ as 3.34e5. Try the question before you open the answer.

  1. Water · 1.0 kg

    Heat the ice until it starts to melt. While it is melting: (a) what happens to the temperature, and (b) what are the particles doing with the energy supplied?

    Show answer

    (a) The temperature stays at 0 °C until all the ice has melted, even though the heater is still on.

    (b) The energy breaks the bonds holding the particles in fixed positions. It increases their potential energy, not their speed, so the temperature does not rise.

  2. Water · 1.0 kg

    Record two readings while the water is a liquid (between 0 °C and 100 °C). Use them to calculate the specific heat capacity of water.

    J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
    Show answer

    c = ΔQ / (mΔθ). Convert ΔQ from kJ to J first.

    For example, if Q goes from 400 kJ to 609 kJ while θ goes from 15.6 °C to 65.6 °C: c = 209 000 ÷ (1.0 × 50) = 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  3. Water · 1.0 kg

    Record a reading just as the ice starts to melt and another just as it finishes. Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion of ice.

    J kg⁻¹
    Show answer

    The energy supplied during melting is about 334 kJ. l = ΔQ / m = 334 000 ÷ 1.0 = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹.

    Use a slow sim speed near the start and end of the flat section, so you can record the readings accurately.

  4. Water · 1.0 kg

    Measure the energy supplied during boiling the same way. How many times longer (in energy) is the boiling section than the melting section? Explain using the particles.

    times
    Show answer

    Boiling: 2260 kJ. Melting: 334 kJ. 2260 ÷ 334 = 6.8 times.

    Melting only loosens the particles so they can slide past each other. Boiling must separate them completely, which takes far more energy.

  5. Water · change the mass to 2.0 kg

    Predict the energy needed to melt the ice, then measure it. What does this tell you about how Q depends on m?

    kJ
    Show answer

    Q = ml = (2.0)(3.34 × 10⁵) = 668 000 J = 668 kJ.

    Doubling the mass doubles the energy needed, so Q ∝ m. A graph of Q against m would be a straight line through the origin.

  6. Lead, then water · 1.0 kg each

    Calculate the temperature rise when 10 kJ is supplied to 1.0 kg of liquid lead (c = 145 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). Then compare the slopes of the liquid sections for lead and water on the heating curve.

    K
    Show answer

    Δθ = Q / (mc) = 10 000 ÷ (1.0 × 145) = 69 K. The same 10 kJ raises 1.0 kg of liquid water by only 10 000 ÷ 4180 = 2.4 K.

    The gradient of θ against Q is 1 / (mc). Lead's c is under the line and is much smaller, so its line is much steeper.

  7. Water · 1.0 kg · set the heater power to 1.0 kW

    Predict how long the ice takes to melt once melting starts. Check by recording readings at the start and end of melting and comparing the times.

    s
    Show answer

    Q = Pt, so t = Q / P = 334 000 ÷ 1000 = 334 s.

    Doubling P would halve the time, because P is under the line in t = Q / P.
