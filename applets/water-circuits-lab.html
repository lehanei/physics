The big idea

Water flows only when the two water surfaces are at different heights. Level surfaces give zero flow, even with the tap wide open. In the same way, current flows only when there is a potential difference.

It is the height of the surface that counts, not where the beaker sits. A beaker raised up but holding little water can be at a lower potential than a low beaker that is nearly full.

Resistance, read from the equation

Q = Δh / R  →  R = Δh / Q

Read as a definition: resistance is the head difference per unit flow. Compare R = V/I: resistance is the voltage per unit current.

Units: units of Δh ÷ units of Q = cm ÷ mL s⁻¹ = cm s mL⁻¹. Compare V ÷ A = Ω.

R = L / (n A)    compare   R = ρL / A
  • As L gets bigger, R gets bigger (L is above the line): R ∝ L.
  • As A gets bigger, R gets smaller (A is below the line): R ∝ 1/A. Since A = πd²/4, doubling the diameter makes A four times bigger and R a quarter.
  • As the number of pipes n gets bigger, R gets smaller (n is below the line). Identical pipes side by side share the flow, like resistors in parallel.

In real narrow pipes, resistance falls even faster with diameter. This lab uses R ∝ L/A so that the pipes behave exactly like wires.

Capacitance

As water moves, A's surface falls and B's rises. Δh shrinks, so the flow slows down and the levels creep together.

C = volume stored / rise in surface height

Read as a definition: capacitance is the volume stored per unit rise in head. Compare C = Q/V: charge stored per unit potential.

Units: mL ÷ cm = cm³ ÷ cm = cm². The capacitance of a beaker is its cross-sectional area. A wider beaker has a bigger C, so more water has to move to change its level by 1 cm.

Try this

  1. Drag the beakers until the water surfaces are level. Read the flowmeters.
  2. Raise A. Watch the flow and the graph as the levels even out.
  3. Double the length, then double the diameter. Compare the flow each time.
  4. Close the tap. Then open it again.

Questions

  1. Set both water surfaces to the same height, but with beaker A's base higher than B's. Does water flow? Explain.
    Show answer

    No. Flow depends on the difference in surface heights, not on where the beakers sit. Equal surfaces give Δh = 0, so Q = 0. Two points at the same potential have no current between them.

  2. Compare flowmeter A with flowmeter B. What does this tell you about current in a single loop?
    Show answer

    They always read the same. Water is not made or lost in the pipe, so the flow rate is the same all along it. Current is the same at every point in a series path because charge is conserved.

  3. With Δh = 20 cm the flow is 10 mL s⁻¹. Calculate the resistance of the pipe and work out its unit.
    Show answer

    R = Δh / Q = 20 / 10 = 2 cm s mL⁻¹. Unit: cm ÷ mL s⁻¹ = cm s mL⁻¹, the head difference per unit flow.

  4. Predict, then test: what happens to the flow if you (a) double the length, (b) double the diameter, (c) use two pipes instead of one?
    Show answer

    (a) R doubles, so the flow halves. (b) A becomes four times bigger, R becomes a quarter, so the flow is four times bigger. (c) R halves, so the flow doubles.

  5. Why does the flow fall as time goes on, even though you change nothing? Describe the shape of the graph.
    Show answer

    A's surface falls and B's rises, so Δh gets smaller and so does Q. The flow drops quickly at first, then more and more slowly. This is the same shape as the current when a capacitor discharges through a resistor.

  6. Make the beakers wider. How does that change the time the levels take to even out? Use the idea of capacitance.
    Show answer

    It takes longer. A wider beaker has a bigger capacitance (more mL per cm), so more water has to move through the same pipe to change Δh. The time depends on R × C, like the time constant RC of a capacitor circuit.

  7. Close the tap. What is the electrical equivalent, and what happens to Δh?
    Show answer

    An open switch. The flow stops at once but Δh stays the same: there can be a potential difference across an open switch with no current through it.

Plugging into potentials

The pump fixes the potentials at the top and bottom. The pipe sections spread the drop between them. The middle beaker sits at whatever potential that split gives it.

The potential divider

Once the middle beaker settles, the same flow passes through both sections:

Q = Δh₁ / R₁ = Δh₂ / R₂  →  Δh₁ / Δh₂ = R₁ / R₂
  • As R₁ gets bigger compared with R₂, Δh₁ gets bigger: the section with more resistance takes the bigger share of the drop.
  • The drops add up to the total: Δh₁ + Δh₂ = htop − hbottom.
hmid = hbottom + (htop − hbottom) × R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)

Why the middle beaker settles

If it is too low, more water flows in than out, so it rises. If it is too high, more flows out than in, so it falls. It stops where flow in equals flow out. While it is filling, the beaker is storing water, like a capacitor charging.

Where the energy comes from

The pump does work lifting water. Each kilogram lifted through Δh gains gΔh joules: energy per unit mass. Compare emf, the work done per unit charge. The water gives that energy up as it flows down through the pipes, as charge does in a resistor.

Try this

  1. With no middle beaker, read the flow. Check that R = Δh / Q matches R = L / (nA).
  2. Add the middle beaker. Make both sections identical and watch where it settles.
  3. Change one section and predict the new middle height before it settles.
  4. Put probes before and after the middle beaker, and on a single pipe in a bundle.

Questions

  1. No middle beaker. Top at 40 cm, bottom at 5 cm, L = 50 cm, d = 6 mm, one pipe. Calculate the resistance and predict the flow. Check with a probe.
    Show answer

    A = π × 6² / 4 = 28.3 mm². R = 50 / 28.3 = 1.77 cm s mL⁻¹. Δh = 35 cm, so Q = 35 / 1.77 ≈ 19.8 mL s⁻¹.

  2. Add the middle beaker with both sections identical. Where does its level settle, and why there?
    Show answer

    Halfway, at 22.5 cm. Equal resistances carrying the same flow have equal drops, so each section takes half of the 35 cm.

  3. Make section 1 twice as long as section 2. Predict the new middle height before it settles.
    Show answer

    R₁ = 2R₂, so section 1 takes two-thirds of the drop: 2/3 × 35 = 23.3 cm. hmid = 40 − 23.3 = 16.7 cm.

  4. Put probes on the pipe into and out of the middle beaker. Compare the readings (a) while it is still filling, (b) once it has settled.
    Show answer

    (a) Flow in is bigger than flow out: the beaker is storing water, like a capacitor charging. (b) They are equal. At a junction in a steady circuit, what flows in flows out.

  5. Give section 2 three pipes. Probe "all pipes" and "one pipe" in that section. How do they compare?
    Show answer

    One pipe carries a third of the total. Parallel paths share the flow, and the flows add up to the total.

  6. Raise the pump lift. What happens to the flows and to the middle beaker?
    Show answer

    All the flows increase in proportion to the total drop. The middle level rises too, but it stays the same fraction of the way down, because that fraction depends only on R₁ and R₂.

  7. In energy terms, what does the pump do? Link your answer to emf.
    Show answer

    It does work lifting water, giving each kilogram gΔh joules. A cell does work on each coulomb of charge: emf is work done per unit charge.

Tanks and lever

Student guide

The diode: a one-way flap valve

WaterElectricity
Flap valveDiode
Spring holding the flap shutSwitch-on voltage (≈ 0.7 V for silicon)
Left tank higherForward bias
Right tank higherReverse bias

Reverse bias pushes the flap onto its seat, so nothing flows. Forward bias pushes the flap away from the seat, but the spring holds it shut until the head difference is big enough. After that the flow rises quickly, and the pipe's own resistance takes over.

The valve sits in a horizontal section so that the flap's weight neither helps nor hinders it. Only the head difference opens it.

well above switch-on: Q ≈ (Δh − Δhon) / R

As Δh gets bigger, Q gets bigger. The slope of the straight part is 1/R, so a bigger pipe resistance gives a flatter line. This is why a real diode always has a resistor in series with it.

The transistor: a lever-lifted gate

WaterElectricity (npn)
Main tankCollector
Outlet tankEmitter
Control tank and thin pipeBase
Control flow IbBase current
Main flow IcCollector current
Lever ratioCurrent gain β
Nozzle heightBase–emitter switch-on (≈ 0.7 V)

A small control flow fills a bucket on the long arm of a lever. The lever lifts a gate in the main pipe and lets a large flow through. The bucket has a hole, so it only stays heavy while control water keeps coming in. Stop the base flow and the gate drops.

β = Ic / Ib

Read as a definition: current gain is the collector current per unit base current. Units: mL s⁻¹ ÷ mL s⁻¹, so β has no unit. As the lever ratio gets bigger, each mL s⁻¹ of control flow lifts the gate further, so β gets bigger.

Ie = Ib + Ic

Both flows end up in the outlet tank, just as base and collector current both leave by the emitter.

Three regions

  • Cut-off: control level below the nozzle, Ib = 0, gate shut. Switch off.
  • Active: Ic = βIb. Small changes in Ib give big changes in Ic. Amplifier.
  • Saturation: gate fully up. The main pipe now limits the flow and more base flow makes no difference. Switch fully on.

Questions

  1. Set Δh negative so the right tank is higher. What happens, and why?
    Show answer

    No flow. The water pushes the flap onto its seat. This is reverse bias: the diode blocks.

  2. Raise Δh slowly from zero. At about what head does water start to flow? What sets this value?
    Show answer

    At about the spring setting (4 cm by default). The spring stiffness plays the part of the switch-on voltage, about 0.7 V for silicon and 0.3 V for germanium.

  3. Record points from −10 cm to +30 cm. Describe the shape of the graph and compare it with the diode characteristic from the LC experiment.
    Show answer

    Zero in reverse, almost zero up to switch-on, then rising sharply and becoming a straight line. Same shape as the forward and reverse I–V curve of a diode.

  4. Well above switch-on the graph becomes a straight line. What limits the flow there, and what is the slope?
    Show answer

    The resistance of the pipe. Q = (Δh − Δhon) / R, so the slope is 1/R. Without a series resistor, almost nothing would limit the current through a real diode.

  5. Transistor: set the control tank below the nozzle. What are Ib and Ic?
    Show answer

    Both zero. No control flow, so the bucket is empty and the gate stays shut. Cut-off: the switch is off.

  6. With β = 50, set Ib to about 0.5 mL s⁻¹. Predict Ic and Ie, then check.
    Show answer

    Ic = 50 × 0.5 = 25 mL s⁻¹ (if the main pipe allows it). Ie = 0.5 + 25 = 25.5 mL s⁻¹.

  7. Keep raising the control tank. Ic stops increasing. Why?
    Show answer

    Saturation. The gate is fully open, so the main head and main pipe resistance now set the flow: Ic = Δhmain / R. The transistor is fully on.

  8. Why does the bucket need a hole in it?
    Show answer

    So that control water has to keep flowing to keep the gate up. A real transistor switches off when the base current stops. Without the hole, the gate would stay stuck open.

  9. Give a real circuit where a small current controls a much larger one.
    Show answer

    For example, a light sensor (LDR) in a potential divider feeds a small base current that switches on a lamp, fan or motor drawing a much larger current.
