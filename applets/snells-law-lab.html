Snell's law and the refractive index of glass

Aim: to verify Snell's law and to determine the refractive index of a glass block. Read the physics first, then work through the bench one step at a time.

Part A · The physics

What the equation tells you

Every equation answers two questions: what does the phenomenon depend on, and how does it depend on it. Then we ask how we test that in the lab.

WHAT?

What does the angle of refraction depend on?

Write the equation for r:

sin r = sin i / n

So r depends on two things: the angle of incidence i and the refractive index n of the glass.

HOW?

How does it depend on them?

  • As i gets bigger, r gets bigger (sin i is above the line).
  • As n gets bigger, r gets smaller (n is below the line): the ray bends more towards the normal.
  • In words: sin i is directly proportional to sin r, sin i ∝ sin r.
  • Careful: i is not proportional to r. The sines are.
HOW?

How do we show it in the lab?

Measure pairs of i and r. Plot sin i on the y-axis against sin r on the x-axis.

Direct proportion gives a straight line through the origin. Compare with y = mx: the slope m is the refractive index n.

The equation as a definition, and its units

n = sin i / sin r = v1 / v2

Definition: the refractive index is the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction. Equally, it is the ratio of the speed of light in the first medium to the speed of light in the second.

Units: units of v1 over units of v2 = m s⁻¹ / m s⁻¹. They cancel, so n has no units. (A sine is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no units either.)

As v2 gets smaller, n gets bigger (v2 is below the line). Slower glass means more bending.

Why does it bend? Thumb and forefinger

The digit that reaches the glass first slows down first. It lags behind, so the wavefront swings round towards the normal. Set n = 1.00: no change of speed, no lag, no bending.

Part D · Check your understanding

Questions on this experiment

The usual questions

Q1Why follow the shadow of a thin pin rather than the edge of the beam?

The shadow of a thin pin is a thin, sharp line. The edge of a beam is wide and fuzzy, so you cannot say exactly where it is. A sharp line lets you place each mark accurately.

Q2Why place the two marks on each ray as far apart as possible?

Each mark has a small error in its position (about ±0.5 mm). Over a long distance between marks, that error makes a smaller difference to the direction of the line. The percentage error in the direction is smaller.

Q3How was the refracted ray found? How was the angle of refraction measured?

Remove the block. Join the marks on the incident side and extend the line to the outline: this gives the point of incidence. Do the same on the emergent side to get the point of emergence. Join the two points: this is the refracted ray. Draw the normal at the point of incidence. Measure the angle between the refracted ray and the normal with a protractor.

Q4Why plot sin i against sin r, and not i against r?

The equation sin i = n sin r says the sines are directly proportional, not the angles. Plotting the sines gives a straight line through the origin. Plotting the angles gives a curve.

Q5How does the graph verify Snell's law?

It is a straight line through the origin. So sin i is directly proportional to sin r, which is Snell's law.

Q6Why does the slope of the graph equal the refractive index?

Compare sin i = n sin r with y = mx. sin i is y, sin r is x, so the slope m is n.

Q7What would you observe if the angle of incidence were 0°?

The ray travels along the normal and passes straight through without bending, so r = 0°. Its speed still changes, but its direction does not. (In the thumb–forefinger model, both digits reach the glass together, so neither lags.)

Q8Why is the angle measured from the normal and not from the surface of the block?

Snell's law is defined using angles to the normal. The normal is the same reference line for the incident and refracted rays at that point.

Q9How does the emergent ray compare with the incident ray?

It is parallel to the incident ray but shifted sideways (laterally displaced). Light bends towards the normal entering the glass and away from the normal by the same amount leaving it. This is a useful check on your drawing.

Q10Give two precautions for this experiment.

Use a sharp pencil and thin lines. Trace the outline of the block carefully and make sure the block does not move. Work in a darkened room so the shadow line is clear. Use large angles of incidence. Repeat for at least six angles.

Q11What are the units of refractive index?

None. n = v1/v2, so its units are m s⁻¹ / m s⁻¹, which cancel.

Percentage error

A protractor reads to the nearest degree, so each angle has an error of about ±0.5°. Each pencil mark is placed to about ±0.5 mm. Use the sliders to see how the size of the measurement changes the percentage error.

Size of the angle

% error = (0.5° / angle) × 100

Percentage error: 2.5%

The same ±0.5° is a bigger fraction of a small angle. This is why large angles of incidence give more accurate results.

Distance the ray travels through the glass

error in direction ≈ (1 mm / length of ray) rad

Error in direction: ±0.95°

P1A student measures r = 12°. Calculate the percentage error.

(0.5 / 12) × 100 = 4.2%

P2Another student measures r = 38°. Calculate the percentage error and compare.

(0.5 / 38) × 100 = 1.3%. The larger angle has about a third of the percentage error.

P3Why would using a smaller angle of incidence lead to a less accurate value of r? (LC 2020)

A smaller i gives a smaller r. The error in reading the protractor stays the same, so it is a larger percentage of a small angle.

P4Why does a thicker block (or a larger angle of incidence) give a more accurate refracted ray?

The ray travels further through the glass, so the points of incidence and emergence are further apart. The same ±0.5 mm error in locating each point makes a smaller error in the direction of the line joining them.
