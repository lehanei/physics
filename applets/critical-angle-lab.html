The critical angle and n = 1/sin C

Aim: to measure the critical angle for a transparent material and to verify that n = 1/sin C. There are two versions: a semicircular glass block, and a beaker of tonic water with a blue laser.

Part A · The physics

What the equation tells you

WHAT?

What does the critical angle depend on?

sin C = 1 / n

Only one thing: the refractive index n of the material (going into air).

HOW?

How does it depend on it?

  • As n gets bigger, sin C gets smaller (n is below the line), so C gets smaller.
  • In words: sin C is inversely proportional to n, sin C ∝ 1/n.
  • Water, n = 1.33: C = 49°. Glass, 1.50: 42°. Diamond, 2.42: 24°.
HOW?

How do we show it in the lab?

Increase the angle of incidence inside the material until the refracted ray just skims along the surface (r = 90°). That angle is C.

Measure C, calculate n = 1/sin C, and compare with the value from Snell's law in Experiment 1.

The equation as a definition, and its units

Critical angle: the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction is 90°.

Read n = 1/sin C as a definition: the refractive index is the reciprocal of the sine of the critical angle.

Units: sin C is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no units, and neither does n.

Beyond C: no light escapes. All of it reflects back inside. This is total internal reflection.

Where the equation comes from

Start from Experiment 1: sin i = n sin r for light going from air into the material.

Light paths are reversible. A ray skimming along the surface in air (i = 90°) enters the material at the critical angle (r = C):

sin 90° = n sin C  ⇒  1 = n sin C  ⇒  n = 1 / sin C

Thumb–forefinger: going from glass into air, the leading digit speeds up and pulls ahead, so the ray bends away from the normal. At C it swings round so far that it runs along the surface.

Part C · Check your understanding

Questions on this experiment

Version 1 · Semicircular glass block

Q1Why is a semicircular block used instead of a rectangular block?

A ray aimed at the centre of the flat face enters the curved side along a radius. A radius is the normal to the curved surface, so the ray does not bend going in. The angle of incidence at the flat face is then set directly by the ray box, and it can be increased past C.

In a rectangular block the ray bends towards the normal as it enters. The angle inside can never reach C, so the light always escapes through the opposite parallel face.

Q2Why must the ray be aimed at the centre of the flat face?

Only a ray aimed at the centre travels along a radius. Any other ray strikes the curved surface at an angle and bends, so the angle at the flat face would not be the angle you set.

Q3How do you know you have reached the critical angle?

The refracted ray just skims along the flat face (angle of refraction 90°). Go any further and it disappears, and the reflected ray inside the block suddenly becomes bright.

Q4Why repeat on both sides of the normal and take the average?

If the normal is drawn slightly off 90°, one side reads too high and the other too low. Averaging cancels this error. Repeating also reduces random error in judging the exact critical position.

Q5What is the main source of error, and how can it be reduced?

Judging exactly when the refracted ray skims the surface: it fades out gradually. Work in a darkened room, use the thin shadow of a pin, rotate the ray box very slowly, and repeat.

Q6What happens if the angle of incidence is greater than C?

No light leaves through the flat face. All of it is reflected back into the glass: total internal reflection.

Version 2 · Tonic water and blue laser

Q7Why is tonic water used?

Tonic water contains quinine, which fluoresces (glows) when blue or ultraviolet light shines on it. The path of the beam inside the liquid becomes visible.

Q8Why a blue laser and not a red one?

Fluorescence needs high-frequency (high-energy) light. Blue light makes quinine glow; red light does not. A laser also gives a narrow beam that is easy to aim and locate.

Q9How is the critical angle found from x and d?

The beam inside the liquid, the wall and the surface form a right-angled triangle. At the incident point the angle between the beam and the vertical normal is C. The side opposite C is x (wall to the midpoint) and the side adjacent is d (depth of the entry point), so tan C = x/d.

Q10Why does it not matter that the beam bends as it passes through the side of the beaker?

Only the path inside the liquid is used. Its two ends, the entry point on the wall and the midpoint of the surface, are measured directly, so any bending outside the liquid has no effect.

Q11Why aim at the midpoint of the surface?

The distance from the wall to the midpoint is half the internal diameter, which is easy to measure accurately. The normal at the surface is vertical everywhere, so any point would work, but the midpoint gives the largest x and so the smallest percentage error.

Q12Give one safety precaution.

Never look into the laser or point it at anyone. Watch for reflections from the glass and the liquid surface.

Q13Why is the value of n slightly higher than for pure water (1.33)?

Tonic water has sugar and quinine dissolved in it, which raise the refractive index slightly.

Percentage error

P1A student measures C = 42° with a protractor that reads to ±0.5°. Calculate the percentage error.

(0.5 / 42) × 100 = 1.2%

P2In Version 2, x = 5.0 cm and d = 4.5 cm, each measured to ±0.05 cm. Calculate each percentage error.

x: (0.05 / 5.0) × 100 = 1.0%. d: (0.05 / 4.5) × 100 = 1.1%.

P3How would a wider beaker improve the accuracy of Version 2?

x and d would both be larger. The same ±0.05 cm reading error is then a smaller percentage of each length.

P4Which error is larger in practice: reading the protractor, or judging the critical position?

Judging the critical position. The refracted ray fades out over a degree or more, which is bigger than the ±0.5° protractor error. That is why the measurement is repeated and averaged.

Exam question on the critical angle

LC 2025 Higher Level · Question 14(b) (28 marks)

National Broadband Ireland is responsible for bringing high-speed fibre to all homes across Ireland. An optical fibre consists of a core made from glass surrounded by cladding made from a different type of glass.

  1. State which glass, Y or Z, would be suitable for the cladding. Justify your answer.
  2. Explain what is meant by critical angle. (12)
  3. Explain, with the aid of a labelled diagram, how a signal is transmitted along the optical fibre.
  4. Calculate the critical angle between the core and the cladding.
  5. Identify the type of glass, from the table, in which light travels fastest. (16)
✓Show the solution

(i) Y. The cladding must have a lower refractive index than the core, so that total internal reflection can happen at the core–cladding boundary.

(ii) The angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction is 90°.

(iii) Light enters the core and strikes the core–cladding boundary at an angle greater than C. It is totally internally reflected, again and again, along the fibre.

(iv) Between two materials, ncore sin C = ncladding sin 90°, so sin C = 1.44 / 1.47 = 0.980, C = 78.4°. (Into air, ncladding = 1 and this becomes sin C = 1/n.)

(v) Y. n = c/v: as n gets smaller, v gets bigger, so the lowest n gives the fastest light.
