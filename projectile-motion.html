Uniformly Accelerated Motion

Projectile motion is the resultant of two motions happening at the same time: a horizontal motion and a vertical motion. Motion in each direction obeys the equations of motion. So before projectiles, we need those equations for motion in a straight line with constant acceleration.

1A special case of UAM

Anything moving freely straight up or down, whether thrown, dropped or falling, is doing uniformly accelerated motion. It is a special case because we always know the acceleration: 10 m s⁻² downwards, caused by gravity. It is the same on the way up, at the very top, and on the way down.

Direction matters, so pick a positive direction and stick to it. On this page, up is positive. Gravity acts down, so a = −10 m s⁻².

Put a = −10 into the two equations of UAM:

v = u − 10ts = ((u + v)/2) t

Positive v: moving up. Negative v: moving down. Positive s: above the start. Negative s: below the start.

Read v = u − 10t as a relationship: for every second the object is in the air, 10 m s⁻¹ is taken off its velocity. That is what an acceleration of 10 m s⁻² downwards means.

4Projectile motion

A projectile is the vertical motion from tab 3, carried sideways at a constant horizontal velocity. The two motions happen at the same time and don't affect each other.

Motion in each direction obeys the equations of motion. So write all four UAM equations twice: once for the horizontal direction (subscript x) and once for the vertical direction (subscript y). Then put in the acceleration for each direction.

Horizontally, sx = uxt, so ux = sx/t: the horizontal velocity is the horizontal displacement per unit time. As t gets bigger, sx gets bigger in direct proportion.

Time is what links the two columns. The same t appears in both. Usually you find t from the vertical motion, then use it in the horizontal motion.
